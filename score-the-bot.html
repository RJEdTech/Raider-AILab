Score the Bot: who taught it to bluff?

    For teachers: the math, the model and the sources

    The math. Expected value (Statistics) and a linear inequality (Algebra 1): answering is worth p × R + (1 − p) × W, and the bot answers when that beats the points for “I don’t know.” With R = 1 and “I don’t know” = 0, the break-even confidence for a penalty of L points is p = L ÷ (1 + L).

    What this toy assumes. The bot is calibrated: when it is 30% sure, it is right on 3 tries in 10. Real chatbots are often more sure than they should be. A wrong answer you believe is set to cost you as much as a right one helps; in real life that cost changes with the task. The 100-question quiz has ten questions at each confidence level from 10% to 100%. All three are choices made for teaching.

    The real research. OpenAI, “Why language models hallucinate” (September 5, 2025, openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate). Its example from SimpleQA: gpt-5-thinking-mini was right 22%, wrong 26% and gave no answer 52%; o4-mini was right 24%, wrong 75% and gave no answer 1%. The authors say tests do not directly cause made-up answers, but most tests score in a way that rewards guessing over admitting uncertainty.

    A human parallel. The SAT used to take off a quarter point for each wrong answer, with five choices per question, so a blind guess netted zero on average. The redesigned SAT dropped that penalty in 2016.

    How to use it in class. It is built to run cold, and it follows Next Word. A good stopping point for discussion is step 5: which rule would you want a chatbot graded by, and who gets to choose? Answers can be copied at the end and pasted into Canvas.
